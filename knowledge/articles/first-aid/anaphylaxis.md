## When to use

Use this when someone has a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), usually within minutes of a food, insect sting or medicine. Signs are swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, trouble breathing, wheezing, a tight throat or hoarse voice, and trouble swallowing.

They may also feel faint, dizzy or confused, have cold, pale, blue or gray skin, and an itchy, raised rash.

## What you need

- Their epinephrine (adrenaline) auto-injector, such as an EpiPen, if they have one
- A phone

## Steps

1. Use their epinephrine auto-injector straight away. Follow the instructions on its side.
2. Call the emergency number. Say you think it is anaphylaxis.
3. Lay them down and raise their legs.
4. If they struggle to breathe, help them sit up slowly instead.
5. If they are pregnant, lay them on their left side.
6. If an insect stinger is still in the skin, scrape it out sideways with a fingernail or card.
7. If they are not better after 5 minutes, use a second auto-injector.
8. Keep checking their breathing and how they respond.
9. If they stop responding and are not breathing normally, start [CPR](article:cpr).
10. Give the used auto-injector to the ambulance crew.

## Warnings

- **Do not let them stand or walk**, even if they feel better.
- Use the auto-injector if you think it may be anaphylaxis, even if the signs seem mild.
- Signs can come back. Another dose can be given every 5 minutes if they don't improve or the signs return.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting to see if it gets worse before using the auto-injector.
- Letting them stand up or walk to the ambulance.

## Get help

Always call the emergency number for a suspected severe allergic reaction, even if they get better after the auto-injector. They need hospital care. People with a severe allergy should carry 2 auto-injectors at all times.
